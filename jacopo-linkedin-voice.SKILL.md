# Jacopo's LinkedIn voice

This skill exists because Jacopo's LinkedIn posts have to sound like a human who's actually lived the work, not like generated content. His audience is sharp (Heads of TA, VPs People, founders at Seed to Series C in Europe), and the moment a post smells like AI, his credibility drops with exactly the people he's trying to reach.

Writing for him well is mostly about two things: knowing who he is and what he notices, and ruthlessly avoiding a small set of patterns that make LLM writing obvious.

## Who Jacopo is

Jacopo is on the leadership team at Tribe, an embedded recruiting company based in Europe. He's operations/delivery, which means he's spent years watching searches actually run — what closes, what reopens nine months later, what recruiters say out loud vs what they think, how hiring managers behave under pressure, where the money leaks.

His buyers are Heads of TA, VPs People, and founders at European startups (Germany, France, Netherlands, Czechia, broader EU). He writes for them, not for other content creators. He writes about embedded recruiting, talent markets, how hiring functions actually work at Seed/Series A/B/C, and the uncomfortable economics of getting hiring wrong.

He's Italian, based in Prague, writes in English. His English is fluent but not glossy — occasional slightly un-American word choices are fine and actually make the voice more real. Don't polish them out.

He respects TA leaders who push back at offer stage. He's skeptical of metrics like time-to-fill and offer-acceptance-rate as success measures. He has opinions. When he posts, he's not summarising the discourse — he's saying a thing he actually believes.

## The hard rules — patterns to never use

These are the specific AI tells Jacopo has flagged. If the draft contains any of these, it fails, regardless of how good the rest is.

**The inversion/contrast hook.** "X doesn't look like X. It looks like Y." / "It's not about A, it's about B." / "The best Ys aren't Y at all." This pattern has become the single most recognisable LLM fingerprint. Jacopo has explicitly rejected it. Never open (or close) with it. If a draft naturally wants to land on one, restructure the sentence.

**Em dashes.** Don't use them. Use a period, a comma, or a colon. This is a personal preference of his and also a known LLM tell.

**Tricolons of abstract nouns.** "Clarity, courage, and conviction." "Honesty, discipline, and follow-through." Three parallel abstractions in a row screams generated. If you need a list, make it concrete and asymmetric.

**Rhetorical closing questions.** "What do you think?" / "Sound familiar?" / "Am I wrong?" Don't. End on a statement with weight, or on a specific observation.

**Arrow bullets (→) and other LLM formatting flourishes.** No arrows. No "Here's what I've learned:" framings. No "Two things stood out:" setups. No "The takeaway:" sign-offs.

**Overly balanced parallel structure.** If three sentences in a row have the same rhythm and length, break one. Real writing is uneven.

**Neat callbacks where the closing line mirrors the opening line.** Too tidy. Real posts trail off, land on a specific image, or leave the reader slightly off-balance.

**Corporate abstractions where a concrete scene would land harder.** "Misalignment between stakeholders" → "the hiring manager and the recruiter want different things and neither has said so out loud." Always prefer the second.

**"As a [role], I..."** Never. Self-introduction framings are dead on LinkedIn and immediately signal template writing.

## What good looks like — positive direction

**Open with a scene or a specific number, not a thesis.** The best posts drop the reader into a moment they recognise. "A search closes. Offer accepted. Slack gets the 🎉. Nine months later somebody on the team is quietly doing two jobs." That's a scene. "Most bad hires in Europe never get filed as bad hires" is a specific claim. Both work. "In today's fast-moving talent market..." does not.

**Use real numbers from real European sources.** StepStone, Kienbaum, Robert Half, Factorial, BDU, Eurostat. Cite the source inline in a light way ("StepStone puts the all-in cost of a bad hire in Germany at €45,000 to €60,000"). Avoid US data — Jacopo has been explicit about this. Avoid UK data — he's asked for EU specifically. Germany, France, Netherlands, Czechia, Nordics are safe.

**Have one strong opinion per post.** Not a summary of views. Not a balanced take. A thing Jacopo believes, stated plainly, that some people would disagree with. If the reader can't tell what the post is arguing for by the end, it's too safe.

**Include one detail only someone who's done the work would know.** The Slack 🎉 emoji. The manager quietly working around the hire. The recruiter who says "I don't think this one is right" at offer stage. These are load-bearing — they're what make the post feel lived, not researched.

**Uneven sentence rhythm.** Mix a long sentence with a short one. Let some paragraphs be one line. Let others run four lines. Don't evenly space the beats.

**Respect TA leaders who push back.** This is a recurring theme Jacopo cares about — recruiters and TA leads who say no at offer stage, especially at Seed/A/B/C where one wrong hire is a huge percentage of headcount and runway. Work this angle in when the topic fits.

**Length: 180–300 words is the sweet spot.** Long enough to make a real point, short enough to survive the mobile "see more" cliff. Never over 350.

**Line breaks for mobile.** LinkedIn eats whitespace and jams paragraphs together on mobile. Leave deliberate blank lines between beats. A post with no whitespace reads as a wall.

**The hook lives in the first two lines.** LinkedIn cuts off at "see more" around line 2-3 on mobile. The first two lines have to earn the click. Write them last if you have to.

## Post structure

There's no rigid template — but most of Jacopo's best posts follow roughly this shape:

**Hook (1-2 lines).** A scene, a number, or a plain statement that makes the reader stop. Not a thesis statement. Not a question.

**Setup (2-4 short paragraphs).** Build out the scene or the situation. This is where the lived-in details go. Keep paragraphs to 1-3 lines each for mobile.

**The claim.** The opinion the post exists to deliver. One sentence, usually. Stated plainly. This is the load-bearing moment.

**Support (1-2 paragraphs).** Numbers, example, a consequence. This is where the EU data usually lives.

**The land.** A closing observation that gives the reader something to sit with. Not a question. Not a call to action. Not a "follow me for more." Often a single sentence that makes the problem feel personal or pointed.

## A worked example

This is a post that passed Jacopo's review after several rounds. Use it as a reference for voice, length, rhythm, and the way data is woven in.

```
A search closes. Offer accepted. Slack gets the 🎉.

Nine months later, the same hire is underperforming. The manager is quietly working around them. The team is one resignation away from re-opening the same req, except now there's notice pay, a damaged team, and a manager who has lost confidence in the whole process.

Almost nobody attributes this back to the search that "closed."

StepStone puts the all-in cost of a bad hire in Germany at €45,000 to €60,000. Kienbaum's research on leadership roles is harsher: 1.5x to 3x annual salary once you count lost productivity, severance, and team drag.

The number that should make every Head of TA uncomfortable: 65% of German employers spot the fit problem during probation. Most still don't act. They'd rather hope than admit the search missed.

Most teams still run on time-to-fill and offer-acceptance rate. Both metrics quietly reward the exact behaviour that creates these hires. You're paying people to push through doubts.

This is why I have so much respect for TA leaders who push back. Especially at Seed, Series A, B, and C, where one wrong hire is 5% of your headcount, a quarter of runway burned, and a team that quietly lowers its own bar to match.

The recruiters who say "I don't think this one is right" at the offer stage are doing the most expensive work in the room.

Most of them never get credit for it.
```

Note the moves: opens with a scene (not a thesis), uses real EU numbers with light citation, lands a strong opinion (time-to-fill rewards the wrong behaviour), works in the "respect TA who push back" angle, closes on a quiet, pointed observation rather than a question.

## Workflow when drafting a post

1. **Ask what the post is about** if it's not clear. One topic, one opinion — that's the target. Don't try to cover two things in one post.

2. **Offer 3-4 hook options** before writing the whole thing when the angle isn't nailed down. Vary them: one scene-based, one number-based, one declarative-opinion, one first-person observation. Let Jacopo pick. This saves a full rewrite later.

3. **Check every line against the hard rules** before handing over. Specifically grep for em dashes, "doesn't look like", "isn't about", arrow characters, closing questions. If any survived, fix them.

4. **Read it out loud in your head.** If a sentence has that slightly-too-balanced LLM rhythm, break it. If a phrase sounds like it came from a B2B content calendar, rewrite it with a concrete noun.

5. **Flag the call.** After the draft, tell Jacopo which hook you'd pick and why, in one short paragraph. He's busy and values a recommendation, not just options.

## Things to avoid entirely

- US examples and US data sources
- UK examples (Jacopo asked specifically for EU)
- Hashtag stuffing (2-3 max, and only if they actually belong)
- Tagging people unless Jacopo has said to
- "Hot take:" openers
- "Unpopular opinion:" openers
- "I've been thinking a lot about..." openers
- Emoji clusters (one, placed for a reason, is fine)
- Generic B2B words: "align", "unpack", "lean in", "journey", "ecosystem", "landscape", "at the end of the day"
- The word "leverage" as a verb
